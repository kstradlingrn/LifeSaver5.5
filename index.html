Hands-on CPR practice

Place the phone face-up beside the manikin, with its front camera able to see your moving upper body. Use landscape orientation, keep it stable and the lens clear. Press Start and begin compressions; no calibration pause is required.

Practice adult hands-only CPR with continuous chest compressions. Follow the metronome for compression pace and follow dispatcher and AED instructions. Use a manikin, never a person.

The AED arrival delay is a training setup. In real care, use an AED as soon as it is available. A helper can operate the phone while you compress.

Use the iPhone’s media-volume buttons to set speaker loudness. The app’s beat-volume control adjusts the sound within that limit.

After a timed session, you can continue for another two minutes. This prototype judges movement rate only, not compression depth, recoil or clinical effectiveness.

Camera view & tracking controls
Processed on this device · not recorded

Tracking uses recurring image motion, which can include unrelated movement. It is not a validated CPR detector. Continue CPR if the image is blocked; a helper may adjust the phone without interrupting the compressor.

Hand/body + image-motion tracking · Place the phone horizontally, face-up and stable beside the manikin’s chest. Keep as much of the moving arms and upper body visible as practical. Allow several seconds of consistent movement. The reader automatically selects a visible body point or consistent moving region for each compressor, then follows complete movement cycles in their strongest direction. The green marker shows its selected focus; it is not a hand-placement guide. Body tracking is checked against local image motion. When body landmarks are cropped, a close-view estimate can use agreement between two automatically selected moving regions; this is a less specific motion estimate, not confirmation of compressions. Slow rates receive two speed-up cues. A switch suggestion requires a previously detected good rate followed by sustained slowing. Good-rate encouragement repeats after each 20 seconds detected at 100–120. Technique/AED reminders occur each minute of detected CPR, with a switch reminder every two minutes. A possible pause of 10 seconds after detected CPR triggers a resume reminder only with fresh, near-static textured camera frames; tracking loss alone is not classified as a pause. Camera estimates cannot confirm a real pause or CPR quality. The last valid rate bridges brief reading gaps (up to 0.75 seconds). “--” appears while establishing or reacquiring tracking; bridged readings never count toward coaching or summaries. AED in use silences coaching and the beat. Keep the moving compressor centered and the phone still. Other repeating movements can resemble compressions; compare estimates with a feedback manikin. Video is processed on this device; depth and recoil are not measured.

Burns

Cool a thermal burn with clean running water.

Recognize

Redness, blistering or damaged skin after heat exposure. Serious burns can cause little pain.

What to do

  1. Stop contact with the heat source safely. Remove jewelry and clothing near the burn only if not stuck.
  2. Cool with clean, cool running water for 5–20 minutes. Keep the rest of the person warm.
  3. Cover loosely with a clean, nonstick dressing. Do not use ice, butter or creams, or break blisters.
Call 911 for deep or large burns, burns affecting the face or airway, or electrical or serious chemical burns. Chemical burns need substance-specific guidance; follow the dispatcher.

(American Red Cross, n.d.-d)

Choking · adult and child

Alternate 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts for severe choking.

Recognize

Unable to speak or breathe, weak or absent cough, or hands at the throat.

What to do

  1. If the person can cough strongly, encourage coughing and watch closely. For severe choking, have someone call 911.
  2. Lean them forward and give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades. Then give 5 inward-and-upward abdominal thrusts, with your fist above the navel and below the breastbone. Repeat.
  3. Use chest thrusts when pregnant or when you cannot encircle the abdomen. If unresponsive, lower safely and start CPR with compressions. Remove only a visible object; never sweep blindly.
Call 911 for severe choking. This summary covers adults and children, not infants.

(American Red Cross, n.d.-a)

Choking · infant under 1 year

For severe choking in a responsive infant, alternate 5 back slaps and 5 chest thrusts.

Recognize

Unable to cry or breathe, a weak or absent cough, or worsening color. If the infant can cough or cry strongly, allow coughing and watch closely.

What to do

  1. Have someone call 911. If alone with a phone, use speakerphone while providing care.
  2. Support the infant face down along your forearm, resting on your thigh. Support the jaw without pressing the throat and keep the head lower than the chest. Give 5 back slaps with the heel of your hand between the shoulder blades.
  3. Support the head and neck as you turn the infant face up along your forearm on your thigh, keeping the head lower than the chest. Give 5 quick chest thrusts with the heel of one hand on the center of the sternum just below the nipple line. Do not use abdominal thrusts.
  4. Repeat until the object is expelled or the infant becomes unresponsive. If unresponsive, place on a firm, flat surface and begin infant CPR with compressions. Remove only an object you can see; never sweep blindly.
Call 911 for severe choking. Chest thrusts for responsive choking are distinct from CPR compressions. Hands-on practice is essential.

(American Heart Association, 2025a)

Seizures

Protect from injury and time the seizure.

Recognize

Convulsions, loss of awareness, unusual repetitive movements or staring.

What to do

  1. Clear nearby hazards and cushion the head with something soft. Do not restrain them or put anything in the mouth.
  2. Time the episode. When safe, place on their side if breathing but not fully awake. Stay nearby and monitor breathing.
  3. If unresponsive and not breathing normally after the seizure, begin CPR and use an AED. Do not give food or drink until fully alert.
Call 911 for a seizure lasting over 5 minutes, repeated seizures without recovery, a first seizure, injury, seizure in water, or trouble breathing.

(American Red Cross, n.d.-g)

Severe allergic reaction

Call 911 and help with prescribed epinephrine.

Recognize

Trouble breathing, swelling of tongue or throat, faintness, or rapidly developing symptoms after an allergen. A rash may be absent.

What to do

  1. Call 911 immediately. Help the person use their prescribed epinephrine device; trained responders may administer according to training.
  2. For an auto-injector, use the outer middle thigh and follow that device’s instructions. Record the time.
  3. Keep them comfortable, limit movement and monitor breathing. Follow dispatcher guidance about repeat medication. Start CPR and use an AED if unresponsive and not breathing normally.
Treat suspected anaphylaxis as an emergency. Antihistamines do not replace epinephrine.

(American Red Cross, n.d.-b)

Cuts and wounds

Protect yourself and control bleeding.

Recognize

Broken skin, bleeding, pain or swelling. Continuous heavy bleeding is an emergency.

What to do

  1. Use gloves if available and apply firm pressure with a clean dressing for bleeding.
  2. For a minor cut once bleeding stops, rinse with clean water and cover with a clean dressing.
  3. Do not pull out an embedded object; press around it. Seek care for deep, dirty, bite or puncture wounds and review tetanus protection.
Call 911 for heavy or uncontrolled bleeding, faintness or signs of shock. Open Stop the Bleed for life-threatening bleeding.

(American Red Cross, n.d.-h)

Sprains and suspected fractures

Rest and support the injury in the position found.

Recognize

Pain, swelling, bruising, deformity or difficulty using the injured area. Movement does not rule out a fracture.

What to do

  1. Limit use. Do not straighten a deformed limb or push exposed bone back in.
  2. For a closed injury, use a cold pack wrapped in a thin towel for no more than 20 minutes; wait 20 minutes before repeating.
  3. Monitor color, warmth and sensation beyond the injury. Keep a suspected head, neck or spine injury still unless movement is needed for safety or lifesaving care.
Call 911 for deformity, exposed bone, numbness or a cold limb, severe injury, or inability to move safely.

(American Red Cross, n.d.-f)

Learning reference for review; not a certification course. In an emergency, call 911 and follow dispatcher instructions. Hands-on skills require training.
Life over limb. Do not delay a needed tourniquet for life-threatening arm or leg bleeding out of fear. Tighten until bleeding stops, secure it, and write the time. A tourniquet may hurt; pain is not a reason to loosen it.
Unsafe scene: get to safety. Do not stay in danger to hold pressure or pack a wound. For life-threatening arm or leg bleeding, rapidly apply an immediately available tourniquet if you can do so without delaying escape, then move yourself and the injured person to safety if you can do so safely. Do not enter an unsafe scene.

Recognize and call for help

Life-threatening bleeding needs immediate action.

Recognize

Blood spurting or flowing continuously, pooling blood, soaked clothing, or a person becoming pale, confused or faint.

What to do

  1. Check scene safety. If the scene is unsafe, prioritize getting to safety; use the rapid tourniquet guidance above when appropriate. Call 911 or send someone to call and bring a bleeding-control kit.
  2. Expose the wound to find the source. Wear gloves if available, but do not delay lifesaving care to find them.
  3. Begin firm direct pressure. Use an available commercial tourniquet promptly for life-threatening arm or leg bleeding. Do not delay a needed tourniquet out of fear; follow your hands-on training and the device instructions. Keep the person warm and monitor breathing.
Call 911 immediately. Look for more than one bleeding site.

(American Red Cross, n.d.-c)

Direct pressure

Press firmly directly over the bleeding wound.

Recognize

Use direct pressure for visible external bleeding while help and equipment are being obtained.

What to do

  1. Place gauze or a clean cloth over the wound and press firmly with your hands.
  2. Maintain uninterrupted pressure. Do not repeatedly lift the dressing to look. Keep the original dressing in place.
  3. If bleeding remains life-threatening, use a tourniquet on an arm or leg, or pack a suitable deep wound if trained. Continue until controlled or a responder takes over.
Call 911 for life-threatening bleeding. For an embedded object, press around it instead of on it.

(American Red Cross, n.d.-c)

Wound packing

Fill a suitable deep wound with gauze, then hold pressure.

Recognize

Packing can help control deep bleeding where a tourniquet cannot be used, such as the groin or shoulder, or a limb when no tourniquet is available.

What to do

  1. Call 911. If trained, expose the wound and use hemostatic gauze if available, or plain gauze or clean cloth.
  2. Pack firmly into the wound against the bleeding source until the cavity is filled. Do not simply lay gauze over the opening.
  3. Hold firm pressure on the packed wound. Follow the hemostatic product instructions and keep pressure until help takes over. Do not remove the packing.
Do not pack the chest or abdominal cavity, an eye, or a wound with an embedded object. Hands-on training is needed for this technique.

(American College of Surgeons, 2025)

Tourniquet · arms and legs

Tighten a commercial tourniquet until the bleeding stops.

Recognize

Use promptly for life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg. Life over limb: uncontrolled bleeding can be fatal. An available commercial tourniquet is a lifesaving tool; follow your training and its instructions.

What to do

  1. Place 2–3 inches above the visible wound toward the heart, never on a joint or on the wound. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions.
  2. Pull the strap tight, twist the windlass until bleeding stops, and secure it. If bleeding persists, add a second tourniquet above the first toward the heart.
  3. Write the application time on the tourniquet’s time strap or another clearly visible location and tell EMS. If you have no marker, remember the time and report it; do not delay bleeding control to find one. Keep the tourniquet visible.
  4. Once applied, first-aid rescuers must not loosen or remove the tourniquet. Hand over to EMS. Removal or conversion is a decision for appropriately trained medical professionals following clinical protocols.
Call 911. Use only on arms and legs; never on the neck or torso. Maintain direct pressure while a tourniquet is being obtained when the scene is safe.

(American Red Cross, n.d.-e)
